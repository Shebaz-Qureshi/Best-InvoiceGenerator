Agency Subcontractor Invoicing: Client Pass-Through Costs and Contractor Payouts

Agencies sit between clients and specialists. Clear rules on markup, pass-through costs, and payment timing keep both relationships healthy.

Modern agencies rarely do everything in-house. A campaign might involve your creative director, a freelance animator, a specialist developer, and a media budget spent on advertising platforms. Each party gets paid in a different way and on a different schedule, yet the client expects a single, coherent invoice that makes sense.

Getting this right protects your margins, your cash flow, and your relationships. This guide explains how to structure the client invoice, decide how transparent to be about subcontractor costs, handle pass-through spending, and pay contractors without financing the entire project yourself.

The three kinds of cost on an agency invoice

Your own fees

Time or deliverables produced by your team, billed at your rates or as part of a fixed fee.

Subcontracted work

Deliverables produced by outside specialists, which you manage and stand behind. You may bill these at cost, with markup, or bundled into a project price.

Pass-through costs

Third-party costs paid on the client's behalf, such as ad spend, stock assets, or printing. These usually need the most careful documentation.

Choose a pricing model before you quote

Fixed project fee

The client pays one price for the outcome. Subcontractor costs are absorbed into the fee, so you do not itemize them. It is simple for the client, but you carry the risk if costs overrun.

Cost-plus

You bill actual subcontractor costs plus an agreed markup. This is transparent and shares risk, but it requires documentation and trust.

Hybrid

A fixed fee for agency work, with pass-through costs billed separately at cost or with a handling fee. Many agencies use this for campaigns with a media budget.

The agency billing chain

  1. The contractor invoices the agency with the agency's PO or project code.
  2. The agency verifies and approves the deliverable.
  3. The agency consolidates the costs into the client invoice.
  4. The client pays, and contractor payouts are settled on schedule.

Presenting subcontractor work to the client

  • Describe the deliverable, not the person who made it.
  • Use the same wording as the approved scope or statement of work.
  • Keep pass-through costs on their own lines with a clear label.
  • Attach or keep on file the third-party invoice supporting each pass-through.
  • Follow the disclosure rules in your client agreement.

Markup: what is reasonable and how to state it

Agencies add markup to subcontracted work to cover project management, quality control, risk, and the cost of sourcing talent. There is no universal percentage, and what is reasonable varies by industry and contract. What matters is that the arrangement is agreed and documented. If the client agreement allows cost-plus billing, state the markup percentage. If you bill a fixed fee, the margin is part of your price, and you do not need to break it out.

Managing cash flow between receipts and payouts

The riskiest position for an agency is paying contractors and media platforms before the client has paid you. To reduce that exposure:

  • Take a deposit or milestone payment before major spending starts.
  • Bill large pass-through costs in advance.
  • Align contractor payment terms with client terms, for example paying a set number of days after you are paid, where the contract allows.
  • Track receivables and payables together so gaps are visible.
  • Set approval limits for costs incurred on the client's behalf.

Paperwork to keep for every project

  • The client contract or statement of work
  • Written approvals of scope changes and budgets
  • Each subcontractor invoice and your payment record
  • Third-party receipts for pass-through costs
  • Your client invoices and payment confirmations

Keep them together, by project, so you can answer any question about where a number came from.

Contracts that make billing smoother

Most billing friction traces back to unclear agreements. Between agency and client, spell out the pricing model, approval process for spending, and markup or handling fees. Between agency and subcontractor, spell out scope, deliverable acceptance, rates, payment timing, and who owns the work. Ask whether the subcontractor's work is confidential, and whether the client may be told who did it. Five minutes on these points at the start saves hours of disputes later.

A quick agency billing checklist

  1. Confirm the pricing model and markup in the contract.
  2. Get written approval for spend before committing on the client's behalf.
  3. Collect approved subcontractor invoices with the right references.
  4. Consolidate into a client invoice with clear categories.
  5. Check that payout dates match your client's payment terms.
  6. File everything by project.

Guidance for subcontractors invoicing an agency

If you are the specialist, make the agency's life easy. Include the agency's purchase order or project code, describe the deliverable in the same terms as the brief, state the amount and currency, and show the payment terms. Send it to the right contact as soon as the work is approved. Clean invoices get paid quickly, and quick payment is the fastest way to become the specialist an agency calls again.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Billing before the subcontractor's work is approved
  • Unclear markup that surprises the client
  • Mixing pass-through costs into fees, which obscures both
  • Paying contractors on terms shorter than the client's, creating a cash gap
  • Missing paperwork for third-party costs

Frequently asked questions

Should agencies disclose subcontractor costs to clients?

It depends on the pricing model in the contract. With a fixed project fee, the client pays for the outcome and individual costs need not be itemized. With cost-plus or pass-through billing, costs are shown, so agree this in advance.

Is it acceptable to add a markup on subcontractor work?

Often yes, if the client agreement allows it. Markup covers management, risk, and the cost of finding and supervising specialists. State the percentage or the pricing model in the contract.

What is a pass-through cost?

A cost you pay on the client's behalf, such as advertising spend, software licenses, or printing, that is billed to the client at cost or with an agreed fee. Show it as a separate line.

How should subcontractors invoice an agency?

With a clear invoice that references the agency's purchase order or project code, describes the approved deliverables, and states amount, currency, and payment terms.

Should I pay subcontractors before the client pays me?

It depends on your terms and your cash position. Many agencies align contractor terms with client terms, for example paying within a set number of days after the client pays, but this must be agreed in writing.

How do I avoid cash flow problems between client receipts and payouts?

Match payment terms, take deposits or milestone payments from clients, and bill large pass-through costs in advance so you are not financing the client's spending.
