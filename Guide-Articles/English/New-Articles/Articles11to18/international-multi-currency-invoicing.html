Multi-Currency Invoicing: How to Bill International Clients Clearly

International invoices go wrong in small ways: an unclear currency, missing bank codes, or an unexplained tax note. Each is easy to prevent.

Working with clients abroad opens up a bigger market, but it also adds friction to the most routine document you send. A domestic invoice can rely on shared assumptions: the currency, the tax rules, the way to pay. An international one cannot. The client's accounts team may be reading it in a second language, converting amounts against their own books, and checking bank details against a different national system.

The fix is not complicated. It is a matter of being explicit about a handful of details. This guide covers the decisions that matter most: currency, exchange rates, bank details, fees, tax notes, and language.

What makes international invoices different

  • Currency: the amount only makes sense if the currency is unambiguous.
  • Payment routes: international transfers use different codes and can involve intermediary banks.
  • Fees: charges can reduce the amount received unless responsibility is agreed.
  • Tax: the treatment of VAT, GST, or sales tax may change across borders.
  • Formatting: dates, decimals, and thousands separators differ between countries.

Choosing the billing currency

Agree the currency in your contract or quote, then use it consistently on every document. There are three common approaches, each with a trade-off.

Your own currency

You avoid exchange-rate risk, but the client bears it and may push back or add a buffer to their own budget.

The client's currency

This is easier for them and can speed up approval, but you take on the exchange-rate risk between invoicing and receiving payment.

A shared major currency

Using USD or EUR is a common middle ground. Both sides share some of the risk, and conversion is often cheaper.

Show the currency clearly

A dollar sign alone is ambiguous, since several countries use dollars. Add the three-letter code, such as USD, CAD, or AUD, next to the total and in the line items. It costs nothing and prevents confusion.

Exchange rates: be explicit

If you invoice in one currency but report in another, the exchange rate matters for your records. If the client pays in a currency different from the invoice, it matters for them too. To keep things clean:

  • State the invoice currency and, if useful, an equivalent in another currency.
  • Name the rate source and date, such as a central bank rate on the invoice date.
  • Agree in the contract who bears movement between invoicing and payment.
  • Keep a record of the rate you used for every cross-border invoice.

Your tax authority may require you to convert foreign income into your local currency for reporting, so keep this documentation with the invoice.

Bank details that actually work

International transfers rely on more identifiers than domestic ones. Include everything the payer's bank will ask for:

  • Account holder name, exactly as registered with your bank
  • IBAN where used, or the account number and routing details
  • BIC or SWIFT code
  • Bank name and country
  • The invoice number as the payment reference

Proofread these carefully. A single wrong digit can cause a rejected payment and days of delay.

Bank fees and short payments

International transfers can pass through intermediary banks that deduct fees along the way. The result is a payment that arrives slightly short, which creates an awkward reconciliation problem. Reduce the risk by stating who covers charges, asking the payer to send the full amount with fees paid by them, and, for smaller amounts, considering alternative payment methods that may cost less than a bank wire. Compare the total cost, not only the headline fee.

Handling cross-border tax notes

Tax rules for cross-border sales depend on where you and your client are based, whether the client is a business or a consumer, and what you are selling. In some cases the service may be outside the scope of your local tax, or the client may account for the tax themselves under a reverse-charge mechanism. In other cases you must charge tax as usual.

  • Do not copy a tax note from another invoice without checking it applies.
  • Include the client's tax registration number if the rules require it.
  • Add a short, accurate explanation of the treatment on the invoice.
  • Confirm the position with a local accountant when you are unsure.

Language and formatting

An invoice that your client's finance team can read is processed faster. If they work in another language, consider a bilingual invoice, or at least translate key terms such as invoice number, due date, and total. Format dates so they cannot be misread, for example writing the month as a word, and use consistent decimal and thousands separators.

International invoicing checklist

  1. Confirm the billing currency against the contract.
  2. Show the currency code next to every amount.
  3. Add complete bank details and the payment reference.
  4. State who pays bank and conversion fees.
  5. Record the exchange rate and its source, if relevant.
  6. Include the correct tax note and registration numbers.
  7. Use an unambiguous date format and the right language.
  8. Open the PDF once to check that every figure is readable.

Frequently asked questions

What currency should I use when invoicing international clients?

Use the currency written into your contract. If none is specified, agree one before invoicing. Billing in the client's local currency makes approval easier, while a stable major currency such as USD or EUR is a common compromise.

Who pays the bank and conversion fees on an international payment?

Whoever you agree on in advance. State it on the invoice, for example that the payer covers their own bank charges so the full amount arrives. Without this, transfers often arrive short.

Do I need to include IBAN and SWIFT details?

For international bank transfers, usually yes. Include your account holder name, IBAN or account number, and BIC/SWIFT code, and double-check them, since a single wrong character can send the payment astray.

Which exchange rate should I use?

Agree the method in your contract, such as the rate on the invoice date, the delivery date, or a monthly average from a named source. Record the rate and source you used so you can explain the figures later.

Do I charge VAT or sales tax on international invoices?

It depends on where you and your client are, what you sell, and whether the client is a business. Rules such as reverse charge and export treatment vary widely, so confirm the correct treatment with a local accountant.

Can I send an invoice in a language other than English?

Yes. Many finance teams process invoices faster in their own language, so consider a bilingual invoice or one in the client's language while keeping numbers and currency codes unambiguous.
