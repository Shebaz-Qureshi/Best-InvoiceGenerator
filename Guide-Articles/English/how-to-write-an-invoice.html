How to Write an Invoice: A Step-by-Step Guide for Beginners

Everything a first-time invoicer needs to get paid without a follow-up email: what to include, in what order, with a worked example you can copy.

Sending your first invoice is usually less complicated than it feels. An invoice is a formal request for payment — a record of what you delivered, what it costs, and when you expect to be paid. Clients pay faster when that record is complete and easy to scan, and slower when they have to email you back to ask what a line item means or where to send the money.

This guide walks through exactly what to include, in what order, using a simple worked example, so you can write a professional invoice in under ten minutes.

What Every Invoice Needs

At minimum, a payable invoice answers four questions: who is billing whom, what was delivered, how much is owed, and when and how to pay. In practice, that breaks down into nine fields:

  • Your business name, address, and a contact email or phone number
  • The client's name and billing details, matching what their accounts team has on file
  • A unique invoice number you won't reuse
  • The issue date and the payment due date
  • A description of each product or service, with quantity and rate
  • A subtotal before tax
  • Any tax, shown as its own line rather than folded into the total
  • The total amount due, in the agreed currency
  • How you accept payment — bank transfer, card, or a payment link

Step-by-Step: Writing Your First Invoice

1. Gather your business and client details

Start with identity, not line items. Write your business name (or your own name if you're not incorporated), the address you use for invoicing, and one reliable way to reach you. Then confirm the client's billing name and address — for larger companies this is often a different contact than the person you worked with, so check who approved the work or ask their accounts payable team directly.

2. Assign an invoice number and set the date

Give the invoice a number that's unique in your records and easy to reference later. A sequential system (1001, 1002) or a dated one (2026-014) both work, as long as you're consistent. Set the issue date to today, then calculate the due date from your payment terms — "Net 15" means 15 days from the issue date, not from when the client happens to open the email.

3. Itemize the work or products

List each deliverable on its own line: what it was, the quantity or hours, and the rate. "Website redesign" tells a client less than "Homepage and 4 interior page layouts, 22 hours at $75/hr." If you agreed on a flat project fee instead of hourly billing, one line with the project name and fee is fine — just make sure it matches what the client approved in writing.

4. Add taxes, discounts, and calculate the total

If you're required to charge sales tax, VAT, or GST, add it as its own line after the subtotal, using the correct rate for your location or your client's, depending on your local rules. Apply any agreed discount before tax, not after. The total due should be the last number the client sees — large, and unambiguous.

5. Set payment terms and accepted methods

State how the client should pay: bank transfer with account details, a card payment link, or a check mailed to an address, and any late fee that applies past the due date if you charge one. A vague line like "payment expected promptly" creates more back-and-forth than a specific date and method ever would.

6. Review, export, and send

Before sending, check the math, confirm the client's name is spelled correctly, and open the exported PDF once to make sure nothing is cut off or misaligned. Send it from an email address the client will recognize, with a subject line that includes the invoice number, so it's easy for both of you to find again.

A Worked Example

Maya runs a one-person design studio and just finished a logo project for a local coffee roaster. Her invoice looks like this:

From
Studio Chen — hello@studiochen.example
Bill to
Riverbend Coffee Roasters, Attn: Accounts
Invoice #
SC-2026-014
Dates
Issued Sep 14, 2026 — Due Sep 29, 2026 (Net 15)
Line item
Logo design package, including 2 rounds of revisions — $850.00
Subtotal
$850.00
Total due
$850.00
Payment
Bank transfer or card link, details in the invoice footer

Nothing here requires the client to ask a follow-up question. The description explains what was delivered, the invoice number is easy to quote back in an email, and the total appears once, clearly.

Common Mistakes First-Time Invoicers Make

  • Using a vague description like "services rendered" instead of naming the actual work
  • Forgetting to include a due date, which leaves "when" up to the client
  • Reusing an old invoice number by accident
  • Leaving tax out of the total, so the amount paid doesn't match what's owed
  • Not saving a copy before sending, in case you need to reissue it
  • Sending an editable Word or spreadsheet file instead of a PDF, which can shift formatting on the client's end

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between an invoice and a receipt?

An invoice requests payment before it's been made; a receipt confirms payment after it has. If a client asks for "proof of payment," they usually want a receipt, not another copy of the invoice.

Do I need to register a business to send invoices?

In most places, no. Many freelancers invoice as individuals using their own name and personal tax ID, at least until their income crosses a registration threshold. Rules vary by country, so check your local requirements if you're unsure.

Do I have to charge sales tax on my invoice?

It depends on where you and your client are based, what you sell, and whether you're registered to collect tax. If you're not sure whether tax applies, it's worth a short conversation with a local accountant before your first few invoices go out.

What should I do if a client doesn't pay on time?

Send a polite reminder that references the invoice number and due date as soon as it's overdue, rather than waiting. A short, specific follow-up gets paid faster than a long one, and most late payments are oversights rather than refusals.

What file format should I send an invoice in?

PDF. It keeps your layout fixed regardless of the device or software the client opens it with, and it's the format most accounting systems expect.

How soon should I send an invoice after finishing work?

As soon as the work or delivery is confirmed complete. Waiting doesn't make the invoice easier to write, and it does delay when the payment clock starts.
